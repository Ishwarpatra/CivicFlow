# Global civic learning content model

CivicFlow’s **Briefings** view is a country-agnostic learning layer. It explains reusable civic concepts—how to frame a public issue, identify the responsible level of government, evaluate sources, participate constructively, and follow an implementation loop. It does **not** claim that a method, institution, deadline, or official source applies in a selected country unless CivicFlow has an explicit, dated local integration for that jurisdiction.

## Editorial boundary

The content is **non-partisan and process-focused**. It does not endorse candidates, parties, legislation, or political outcomes. It helps a visitor reason about public decisions and problem-solving without telling them which political position to adopt.

| Learning card | Purpose | CivicFlow boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Read the decision** | Identify the public problem, decision-maker, affected people, and evidence that would change the answer. | A framework, not advice for a particular law or country. |
| **Participate with a record** | Match a question or evidence submission to an appropriate public process, then record the response. | The visitor must confirm the local channel, deadline, and eligibility with an official authority. |
| **Work through an issue** | Use a small, measurable loop: name the local problem, test a practical action, collect feedback, and adjust. | CivicFlow does not represent this as an official government programme. |
| **Close the loop** | Track what was promised, what happened, and which unresolved questions need follow-up. | No claim of outcome, enforcement power, or connected local record. |

## Why these patterns

The OECD’s citizen-participation guidance describes participation across problem definition, policy development, implementation, and evaluation, and emphasizes clear outcomes, appropriate methods, accessible communication, feedback, and evaluation.[1] The UN frames participation as meaningful involvement in matters that directly affect people and identifies consultations, multi-stakeholder forums, participatory budgeting, and community-driven development as common strategies.[2] The World Bank’s description of problem-driven iterative adaptation stresses locally prioritized problems, trying different approaches, feedback loops, and adapting learning to context.[3]

> “CivicFlow offers an educational process map, not a substitute for local civic authority, legal advice, or verified country-specific political information.”

## Product rules

Every learning card must state its scope in plain language. The location selector continues to label dynamically searched places as **Context preview**. The guide must continue to reject non-civic requests and apply its server-side request allowance before contacting the AI provider. Learning content should link to its sources directly rather than extracting country-specific claims from them.
